## The question

How do faculty, researchers, and graduate students from different racial backgrounds in health sciences and STEM fields use and perceive the University of Michigan Library, its collections, research support, and instruction support? Where might the library be able to make its services more inclusive?

## The project

In 2024, I completed a data internship through the National Center for Data Services (NCDS), part of the Network of the National Library of Medicine. Working with the University of Michigan Library's winter 2023–24 campus-wide survey, I examined responses about library use and perceptions, with particular attention to differences across racial groups. I used statistical analysis and data visualizations to explore patterns in the responses and to consider what they might mean for library services.

## What I found

Survey respondents generally rated the library positively, and my analysis did not find statistically significant differences in library use or perceptions across the racial groups I examined. But the descriptive results raised questions worth exploring. A smaller percentage of white respondents rated the library as **very welcoming**, while a smaller percentage of Black respondents rated it as **very respectful of diversity**, relative to the other groups compared. I also observed gender differences in reported belonging and safety.

These findings required caution. Some racial and ethnic groups were represented by very small numbers of respondents, limiting meaningful comparisons; I concentrated the statistical analysis on white, Asian, and Black respondents. The results suggested areas for further investigation, rather than establishing explanations for the differences.

## Why it matters

I connected these patterns to the *Inclusion for All* framework described by Brannon and colleagues (2018), which considers how diversity initiatives can foster inclusion while minimizing backlash. My recommendations emphasized choice and autonomy, structured opportunities for interaction across groups, and education about the continuing effects of racial inequality. The goal was to help academic libraries think more carefully about how different people experience their spaces, collections, and services.
